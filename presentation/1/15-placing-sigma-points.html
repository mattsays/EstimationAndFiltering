Placing the Sigma Points & Assigning Weights Mathematically
Where they go, how much they weigh, and why a small \(\alpha\) helps when \(\mathbf{f}\) is strongly nonlinear
1. Place along \(\mathbf{L}\) axes
\(\mathbf{L}\) is a square root of \(\mathbf{P}\): it turns a unit circle into the uncertainty ellipse.
\(\displaystyle \mathbf{P}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\)
Sigma points: \(\gamma\) steps along the axes, pushed through \(\mathbf{L}\).
→
2. The \(2n+1\) sigma points
\(\mathbf{x}_0=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), and for \(i=1,\dots,n\) two points:
\(\displaystyle \mathbf{x}_i=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm\Big(\sqrt{(n+\lambda)\mathbf{P}}\Big)_i=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm\gamma\,(\mathbf{L})_i\)
\(\displaystyle \gamma=\sqrt{n+\lambda},\qquad \lambda=\alpha^2(n+\kappa)-n\)
→
3. Weights
\(w_0, w_i\) give the mean. For the covariance, the central point gets a corrected weight \(w_0^c\):
\(\displaystyle w_0=\frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda},\quad w_i=\frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\)
\(\displaystyle w_0^c=w_0+1-\alpha^2+\beta\)
Why choose a small \(\alpha\)?
Sigma points are probes of \(\mathbf{f}\). The parameter \(\alpha\) is the knob that sets how far from the mean they sit.
Large \(\alpha\): probes land far away, where \(\mathbf{f}\) may look nothing like it does near \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\).
Small \(\alpha\): probes stay where the distribution actually lives, so they capture the local behavior.
The more strongly \(\mathbf{f}\) bends, the smaller \(\alpha\) should be.
