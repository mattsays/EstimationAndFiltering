Placing the Sigma Points
Where they go, and why a small \(\alpha\) helps when \(\mathbf{f}\) is strongly nonlinear
1. Square root of \(\mathbf{P}\)
Factor the prior covariance. The columns of \(\mathbf{L}\) are the directions the points spread along.
\(\displaystyle \mathbf{P}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\)
→
2. The \(2n+1\) sigma points
\(\mathbf{x}_0=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), and for \(i=1,\dots,n\) two points:
\(\displaystyle \mathbf{x}_i=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm\Big(\sqrt{(n+\lambda)\mathbf{P}}\Big)_i=\hat{\mathbf{x}}\pm\gamma\,(\mathbf{L})_i\)
\(\displaystyle \gamma=\sqrt{n+\lambda},\qquad \lambda=\alpha^2(n+\kappa)-n\)
→
3. Weights
\(w_0, w_i\) give the mean. For the covariance, the central point gets a corrected weight \(w_0^c\):
\(\displaystyle w_0=\frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda},\quad w_i=\frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}\)
\(\displaystyle w_0^c=w_0+1-\alpha^2+\beta\)
Why a small \(\alpha\)?
The points sample \(\mathbf{f}\) at \(\boldsymbol\delta=\pm\gamma(\mathbf{L})_i\), so \(\|\boldsymbol\delta\|\sim\gamma\):
\(\displaystyle \mathbf{f}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\boldsymbol\delta)=\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol\delta+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^T\mathbf{H}\boldsymbol\delta+\cdots\)
The neglected high-order terms grow with \(\gamma=\alpha\sqrt{n+\kappa}\). A small \(\alpha\) keeps the sampling local, which matters most when \(\mathbf{f}\) bends strongly.
Large \(\alpha\): points far from \(\hat{x}\)
Points land on the flat tails: the secant slope (orange) misses the local slope at \(\hat{x}\) (green).
Small \(\alpha\): points stay local
Points stay near the mean: the secant (orange) matches the local slope (green).
