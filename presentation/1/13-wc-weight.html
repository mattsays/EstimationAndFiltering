What is \(w_0^c\)?

Encoding higher-order moment information into the covariance \(\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\)

1. The Truncation Problem

Non-linear functions \(f_k(\mathbf{x})\) distort the higher-order moments (e.g., skewness, kurtosis) of the prior distribution.

\(\displaystyle \begin{array}{ccc} \textbf{Prior} & \xrightarrow{\ f_k(\mathbf{x})\ } & \textbf{Distorted} \\ (\text{Skew} = 0) & \longrightarrow & (\text{Skew} \neq 0) \\ (\text{Kurtosis} = 3) & \longrightarrow & (\text{Kurtosis} \neq 3) \\ \vdots & & \vdots \end{array} \)

2. The Solution

We compensate for the distorted kurtosis by tuning only the central weight \(w_0^c\). This safely adjusts the covariance spread without affecting the first moment (the mean \(\mathbf{\hat{x}}\) never moves!).

→

3. The Mathematical Formula

The central covariance weight adds a specific compensation term:

\(\displaystyle w_0^c = w_0 + (1 - \alpha^2 + \beta) \)

What is \(\beta\)?

The Unscented Transform is exact up to the 3rd moment, but truncation errors appear at the 4th moment (kurtosis). So we need to fix the taylor truncation error!

\(\beta\) directly encodes this missing shape information. For a Gaussian prior, setting \(\beta = 2\) rigorously neutralizes this 4th-order error.

Geometric Intuition: Tuning the Covariance The extra term acts as a "dial" that safely inflates or deflates the covariance spread \(\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\) to capture non-linear distortion, while keeping the mean \(\mathbf{\hat{x}}\) exactly anchored.
